Your Child’s First Dental Visit

The best time for a first visit is when the first tooth appears and no later than the first birthday. At this age, the visit is short and gentle. We look at the teeth and gums, talk to you about feeding, cleaning and habits such as thumb sucking, and let your child get used to the chair.

For older children, the first visit is mainly about trust. We explain each step in simple words, let them touch and see the instruments and never force treatment. A calm first visit makes every later visit easier.

Preventing Cavities in Milk Teeth

Milk teeth matter. They hold the space for adult teeth, help with chewing and speech and affect how a child smiles. Decay in milk teeth can cause pain and infection, and it can affect the adult teeth coming through.

  • Start brushing as soon as the first tooth appears, with a smear of fluoride toothpaste.
  • Help your child brush until they can tie their own shoelaces, which is roughly around the age of seven or eight.
  • Avoid putting a baby to bed with a bottle of milk or juice.
  • Keep sweets and sugary drinks to meal times.
  • Book check-ups every six months.

We can also apply fluoride and fissure sealants to protect new molars from decay.

Fillings and Other Treatment for Children

If a cavity does develop, treating it early keeps the procedure short and simple. We numb the area gently, explain what we are doing and work at your child’s pace. Parents are welcome to stay close by.

Where a milk tooth is badly decayed, we may recommend a crown or, in some cases, extraction. We will explain why and what it means for the adult teeth. If a tooth is knocked out or broken in a fall, call us immediately.

Thumb Sucking, Bottle Feeding and Other Habits

Most young children grow out of sucking their thumbs or using a dummy. If the habit continues beyond the age when adult teeth start to appear, it can push the front teeth forward or affect the way the jaws develop. We advise gently and without scolding: praise, distraction and small rewards work better than punishment.

Mouth breathing, tongue thrust and prolonged bottle feeding can also affect the teeth and jaws. If you notice any of these, mention them at the visit, and we will guide you.

Early Orthodontic Check-ups for Children

Many orthodontic guidelines suggest a first check around the age of seven, when the first adult teeth are coming through. This does not mean braces at seven. It means an early look at how the jaws and teeth are developing, so that problems can be spotted while treatment is simpler.

Children with crowded teeth, a bite that does not meet properly, thumb sucking or early loss of milk teeth may benefit from a review. Helping an Anxious Child

Some children are afraid of the dentist, usually because of fear of the unknown. You can help by speaking about the visit in positive words, not promising that it will not hurt, and avoiding words such as pain, needle or drill. Let us do the explaining, in language that children understand.

We take time over the first visit, use gentle techniques and let your child have breaks. Parents who feel nervous themselves can mention it to us, because children pick up on our feelings. A relaxed, friendly first experience is the best start to a lifetime of healthy teeth.

Diet, Snacks and Healthy Teeth

What your child eats and how often matters as much as brushing. Every time sugar reaches the teeth, bacteria produce acid for about twenty to thirty minutes. Frequent snacking means constant acid. Offer sweets with meals instead of between them, choose water over juice and fizzy drinks, and avoid sticky sweets that cling to the teeth.

Cheese, milk, nuts, fruit and vegetables are better snacks. Chewing sugar-free gum after meals helps older children, as it increases saliva. Make brushing before bed a habit, and nothing but water afterwards.

Injuries, Sports and Mouthguards

Falls and sports injuries are a common cause of broken teeth in children. If a tooth is knocked out, find it, hold it by the crown and call us at once. A permanent tooth can sometimes be saved if it is replaced quickly. Do not try to replace a milk tooth.

For contact sports such as cricket, football and martial arts, a well-fitting mouthguard helps protect the teeth. Children with braces especially benefit from one. Ask us about suitable options for your child.

Teething, Eruption and What to Expect

Babies usually get their first tooth at around six months, though it can be earlier or later, and the full set of twenty milk teeth is usually in by about three years. Teething can cause drooling, irritability and a desire to chew. A cold teething ring and a gentle gum massage may help. Rubbing numbing gels on a baby’s gums is not advisable without your dentist’s advice.

Adult teeth start to appear around six years of age, with the first molars behind the milk teeth and the lower front teeth. Between about six and twelve, children have a mix of milk and adult teeth, which can look crowded and uneven. This is the stage when an orthodontic check helps most.

Questions about dentist for kids

When should my child first see a dentist?

Ideally when the first tooth appears and no later than the first birthday. Early visits are short and mostly about prevention and advice for parents.

Do milk teeth really need fillings?

Yes. Milk teeth hold space for the adult teeth and decay in them can cause pain and infection. Treating them protects your child’s comfort and the teeth that follow.

How do you handle anxious children?

We take our time, explain what we are doing in simple language and let the child get comfortable first. Parents are welcome to stay close by.

How often should my child visit?

Usually every six months, or sooner if you notice dark spots, pain or sensitivity.
