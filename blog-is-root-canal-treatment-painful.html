Is Root Canal Treatment Painful? What to Expect

12 October 2026 · Noida Dental Hospital

The short answer

The treatment itself is done under local anaesthesia, so most people feel pressure and vibration but not pain. The pain people associate with root canals usually comes from the infection that makes the treatment necessary, not from the treatment.

What happens during the procedure

Your dentist numbs the tooth, removes the infected pulp from inside it, cleans and shapes the canals, and then fills and seals them. A crown or strong filling is usually placed afterwards to protect the tooth.

What you may feel afterwards

Mild tenderness when biting is common for a few days. It usually settles with the pain relief your dentist recommends. Contact the clinic if you have severe pain, swelling or fever.

Why not just pull the tooth?

A natural tooth is usually the best chewing tooth you can have. Removing it can lead to a gap, shifting teeth and the need for an implant or bridge later. If a tooth can be saved, it is generally worth saving.

If you have tooth pain, do not wait. Early treatment is simpler and more comfortable.

Where the Root Canal Reputation Comes From

Ask people about root canals and many will say they are painful. The reputation dates from a time when anaesthesia was less effective and when people came for treatment only after enduring days of severe pain. Today, the picture is different. Good local anaesthesia, careful technique and earlier diagnosis make the procedure comparable to having a deep filling.

The confusion also comes from how toothache feels. People associate the pain they felt before treatment with the treatment itself, when in fact the treatment is what ends it.

Signs You May Need One

  • Pain that lingers after hot or cold food and drink.
  • A throbbing tooth that keeps you awake.
  • Pain on biting or chewing.
  • A darkened tooth.
  • Swelling or a bump on the gum.
  • A tooth that is sensitive to touch.

If you have any of these, do not wait. The sooner the tooth is examined, the simpler the treatment is likely to be.

Recovery and Aftercare

Expect some tenderness for a few days. Avoid chewing hard food on that side until the tooth has its final filling or crown, take medication as advised and keep cleaning the area gently. Most people return to their normal routine the next day.

If pain gets worse, swelling develops or your bite feels high, call the clinic. These are usually simple to resolve.

Why Saving the Tooth Is Usually Worth It

A natural tooth is almost always the best chewing tooth you can have. If a tooth is extracted, the gap can let neighbouring teeth drift and the bite change. Replacing it with a bridge or implant costs more time and money. If the tooth can be saved, a root canal and crown are usually the best long-term option.

Not every tooth can be saved. We will explain honestly when a tooth is not worth treating and what the alternatives are.

More Questions About Root Canals

How long does a root canal take? Many take one or two visits, with each visit lasting about an hour or more, depending on the tooth. Can I go to work afterwards? Most people can, once the numbness has gone. Can I eat afterwards? Wait until the numbness wears off, and chew on the other side for a few days.

Will I need a crown? Often yes, especially for back teeth. Can a root canal fail? Rarely, and it can sometimes be repeated. What is the alternative? Extraction and replacement, which usually costs more time and money.

Is it safe? Yes. Root canal treatment is a well-established, routine procedure. If you are worried, tell us, and we will explain each step before we begin.

Root Canal vs Extraction vs Doing Nothing

Doing nothing is not a real option when a tooth is infected, because the infection can spread to the bone, face and, rarely, further. Extraction ends the infection but leaves a gap. A root canal saves the tooth and ends the infection.

If a tooth is extracted, think about the long term. A gap can let teeth shift, and a replacement is another procedure. A Closer Look at Each Step

First, the dentist numbs the tooth and the surrounding gum. A small rubber sheet is often placed around the tooth to keep it clean and dry. An opening is made through the top of the tooth to reach the pulp chamber. The infected pulp is removed with fine instruments, and the canals are measured and shaped.

The canals are then rinsed with a disinfecting solution, dried and filled with a rubbery material that seals them. The access opening is closed with a filling, and the tooth is later covered with a crown. Each step is designed to remove infection and prevent it from returning.

If the Idea Still Worries You

Fear of dental treatment is common, and there is no shame in it. Tell the dentist how you feel, and ask them to explain each step. Ask for a signal to pause. Choose a time of day when you feel calm, and bring someone with you if that helps.

A clinic with a gentle approach will go at your pace. Our painless dentistry page explains how we help nervous patients, and it applies to root canals as much as anything else.

What Affects the Cost?

The cost depends on the tooth, the number of canals, the complexity, any retreatment and the crown. Front teeth usually have one canal, and back teeth up to four, which means more time. We give you an estimate after an examination and X-ray, and we explain what it includes.

If you are comparing clinics, check whether the crown is included. A root canal without a crown can leave the tooth at risk of cracking. Look for clarity, as you would with an affordable orthodontist near me, and do not choose on the lowest price alone.
